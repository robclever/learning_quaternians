01 / The problem

What is gimbal lock?

Imagine a camera carried by three nested rings. Each ring gives you a rotation control: yaw about Z, pitch about Y, and roll about X. Normally, their combined motions let you adjust orientation in three independent directions.

Three controls

Euler angles describe a sequence of turns. Here the outer yaw ring carries the pitch ring, which carries the roll ring. Turning an outer ring also moves the axes inside it.

Two axes align

At pitch +90° or −90°, the roll axis lies on the yaw axis. Those controls now turn about the same line. They cannot provide three independent small rotation adjustments.

One direction is lost

The object still has a valid orientation. The trouble is the controls: different roll/yaw settings can describe exactly the same pose. A camera pointing vertically or a three-ring platform can encounter this configuration.

Why quaternions help: software can store and update orientation directly with a unit quaternion, avoiding this singularity in Euler coordinates. A quaternion does not mechanically unlock physical rings, and converting back to Euler controls brings the singularity back.

    03 / A different representation

    Quaternions, without the math refresher

    Imagine holding a toy airplane. You want to describe which way it is pointing and how much it is tilted. That is its orientation. Its position in the room does not matter here.

    Euler angles: three turning instructions

    You could say, “Turn this far left, tip this far up, then roll this far.” Those are the yaw, pitch, and roll controls we used above.

    The catch is that these turns affect the directions of the later controls. When two control axes line up, you effectively have two knobs doing the same job.

    Another way: one imaginary skewer

    Push an imaginary skewer through the airplane’s center. Point that skewer in the right direction, then turn the airplane around it.

    Starting from a reference pose, you can reach any final orientation with one carefully chosen skewer direction and one amount of turn.

    A quaternion: four numbers storing that turn

    A rotation quaternion is a package of four numbers that encodes that same skewer-and-turn idea.

    The numbers are arranged to make it easy for a computer to combine turns and smoothly move between orientations. You generally let a math library calculate them.

    So what do the four numbers mean?

    We label them (x, y, z, w). The first three point along the imaginary skewer, but their size also depends on the amount of turn. The last number, w, helps encode that amount. They work together: x, y, and z are not three separate turn angles, and w is not a fourth direction.

    You do not have to read a quaternion and immediately picture the airplane. Think of these numbers as a useful storage format. The animation is what makes the stored orientation understandable.

    A concrete example

    Start with the airplane level. Put the skewer along its left-to-right axis—the Y axis in this demo—and turn it a quarter-turn, or 90°. The quaternion for that turn is approximately:

    q = (0, 0.707, 0, 0.707)

    Do not worry about memorizing 0.707. The useful point is that this is a perfectly ordinary set of numbers, even at the pose where our Euler controls lined up. A slightly larger turn gives a slightly different quaternion.

    How do the 3D axes map into quaternion numbers?

    The airplane still lives in ordinary 3D space. A quaternion is not a new spatial frame with a fourth direction. It describes the rotation between two frames: here, the airplane’s local axes and the fixed world axes.

    Use capital X, Y, Z for spatial axes, and lowercase x, y, z, w for the four stored quaternion components. If the rotation axis is the unit vector u = (uX, uY, uZ), the mapping is:

    x = uX sin(θ/2)
    y = uY sin(θ/2)
    z = uZ sin(θ/2)
    w = cos(θ/2)

    So a rotation axis pointing along X contributes to x; an axis along Y contributes to y; an axis along Z contributes to z. A tilted axis contributes to several components. All three are scaled by the same half-angle factor.

    Four examples of a positive 90° turn: sin(45°) = cos(45°) ≈ 0.707
    Spatial rotation axisUnit direction uQuaternion (x, y, z, w)
    X(1, 0, 0)(0.707, 0, 0, 0.707)
    Y(0, 1, 0)(0, 0.707, 0, 0.707)
    Z(0, 0, 1)(0, 0, 0.707, 0.707)
    Halfway between X and Y(1/√2, 1/√2, 0)(0.5, 0.5, 0, 0.707)

    The last row is one turn around a diagonal axis. It is not a 90° X turn followed by a 90° Y turn. Those sequential turns would need quaternion multiplication.

    Where do the airplane’s axes point after the turn?

    This is a different question from choosing the rotation axis. The airplane has three local directions: X = forward, Y = sideways, and Z = the remaining perpendicular direction. To find each direction in world coordinates, rotate it with the same quaternion.

    For this demo, q maps a local vector into the world frame. Put a vector v = (vX, vY, vZ) into a quaternion with w = 0, then apply:

    p = (vX, vY, vZ, 0)
    p′ = q p q⁻¹
    world vector = the first three components of p′

    Here q⁻¹ means the inverse of q. For a unit quaternion in our (x, y, z, w) order, q⁻¹ = (−x, −y, −z, w). Multiplication uses quaternion rules, not component-by-component multiplication. To map a world vector back into the local frame, reverse the operation: q⁻¹ p q.

    Our +90° Y-axis example: q = (0, 1/√2, 0, 1/√2)
    Local direction before the turnWorld direction after q p q⁻¹What happened?
    X = (1, 0, 0)(0, 0, −1) = −ZThe airplane’s forward direction now points along world −Z.
    Y = (0, 1, 0)(0, 1, 0) = +YThe rotation axis itself stays fixed.
    Z = (0, 0, 1)(1, 0, 0) = +XThe third local axis turns along with the airplane.

    These three output vectors are the columns of the rotation matrix shown in the readout. For this example:

    R = [ 0 0 1 ]
    [ 0 1 0 ]
    [−1 0 0 ]

    R × (a, b, c) = (c, b, −a)

    For example, a point at local (2, 1, 0) rotates to world (0, 1, −2), assuming the origins coincide. Rotation changes its direction without changing its distance from the center. Translation—moving the airplane’s center—is a separate operation.

    Connect this to the pictures: in the side-view sketch, +Y points toward you, +X points right, and −Z points up. That is why the nose moves from right to up. In section 4, the orange, green, and blue lines show the rotated local X, Y, and Z axes respectively. They stay perpendicular in 3D, even when projection makes their screen angles look different.

    Why does that help with gimbal lock?

    Go back to experiment 3: two angle readings changed, but the airplane stayed still. The problem was how those controls described the motion.

    With quaternions, the computer keeps the airplane’s orientation and combines it with whatever turn you ask for next. It does not have to untangle a separate roll and yaw at the vertical pose first. You can still request a small turn around any spatial direction.

    The important distinction: quaternions avoid the problem in how software represents and updates orientation. They do not repair a physical set of rings that has locked, and converting back to Euler angles still gives ambiguous roll/yaw readings at that pose.

    And why are they useful for animation?

    Suppose you save two airplane poses and want to move smoothly between them. A method called SLERP fills in the poses along a shortest rotation path. With steady playback, the airplane turns at a steady speed instead of separately adjusting three angle controls.

    In section 4, drag the slider from 60° to 120°. Watch the airplane pass through 90° and keep turning. Passing through 90° alone is possible with Euler angles too; the quaternion advantage is that further rotation updates do not rely on the Euler controls staying independent.

    Optional: where do those numbers come from?

    Use a direction vector u for the skewer, scaled so its length is 1. Let θ (theta) be the angle you want to turn. The recipe is:

    (x, y, z) = u × sin(θ/2)
    w = cos(θ/2)

    For our Y-axis example, u = (0, 1, 0) and θ = 90°. Half the angle is 45°, and both sin(45°) and cos(45°) are about 0.707. That gives (0, 0.707, 0, 0.707).

    The four numbers obey x² + y² + z² + w² = 1. This makes it a unit quaternion. The constraint is why four stored numbers do not mean four independent rotation controls.

    The half-angle comes from the way a quaternion is applied: the vector being rotated is multiplied on both sides, as q p q⁻¹. This operation turns it by twice the angle encoded inside q.

    You may see q written as w + xi + yj + zk. That is another notation for the same four components. Also, reversing all four signs gives the same orientation: q and −q describe the same turn.

    To combine turns, multiply their quaternions. Order matters, just as tipping an airplane and then rolling it can give a different result from rolling it and then tipping it.

    04 / Through the singularity

    A quaternion keeps turning through 90°

    Same vertical pose, continuous motion

    Drag across 90°. The white vehicle moves smoothly. These colored lines are the vehicle’s body axes, which stay perpendicular in 3D; they are not the nested gimbal control axes above.

    At θ = 90°, q ≈ (0, 0.707, 0, 0.707) in (x, y, z, w) order. No component becomes undefined, and the next rotation is still well-defined.

    This example blends the 60° and 120° endpoint quaternions with SLERP. A simple Euler pitch sweep can also pass through 90°; the benefit is that quaternion updates do not depend on three Euler controls remaining independent.

    In practice: keep orientation as a unit quaternion, compose rotation updates with quaternion multiplication, and normalize when numerical drift requires it. Use Euler angles for display when useful, knowing they are ambiguous at the singularity.

    Convention: R = Rz(yaw) Ry(pitch) Rx(roll). Applied to a vector, the rightmost rotation acts first. Geometry and numerical readouts are calculated in Rust; both demonstrations work offline.
